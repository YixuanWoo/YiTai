The lighthouse stood at the end of a spit of black rock, and the keeper climbed its one hundred and forty steps twice a day, once before dawn and once after dusk(**〔1〕His knees had been complaining for years, but he never once mentioned it to anyone.**). Nobody in the village below could remember a night when the lamp had failed, and this was not because the keeper was a careful man, but because he was a stubborn one. He had a rule, written on the back of an envelope and pinned above his stove, that said the lamp is lit or I am dead, and in thirty-one years he had never found occasion to amend it.

Visitors were rare and were received the way weather is received, which is to say noticed, discussed, and not invited in. He would stand in the doorway with his hands behind him and answer questions in the shortest form the language allowed(**〔2〕The shortest form was often a single word, which the villagers took for rudeness and the children took for mystery.**), and when the questions ran out he would say the tide, and close the door.

There was one exception. A girl of perhaps nine came up the path every second Thursday with a tin of soup, and he let her in without asking anything at all(**〔3〕She never explained why she came, and he never asked, and this arrangement had held for four years.**). She would sit at his table and read the almanac aloud while he mended the wick, and if she mispronounced a word he would correct her, and if she pronounced it correctly he would say nothing, which she had learned to interpret as praise.
